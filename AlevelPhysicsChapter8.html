Superposition — waves adding up

Everything in this chapter is one idea repeated: when two waves meet, add their displacements. Stationary waves, harmonics, interference, double slits, gratings — all of them are that single sentence applied to a different geometry. Play with each one until you see the adding happen.

01 The principle of superposition

Two waves pass through the same point. At that instant, the resultant displacement is just the sum — with signs. That's it. Both waves then carry on completely unchanged.

Add two waves, point by point green = the sum
resultant amplitude ≈ 2.00 constructive — maximum
Definition

Principle of superposition — when two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements of the waves at that point.

Note the word displacement, not "amplitude" and not "intensity". Amplitudes only add when the waves happen to be in phase.

Trap

Constructive interference needs waves in phase (phase difference 0, 360°, 720°…). Destructive needs them antiphase (180°, 540°…). If the two amplitudes are equal, destructive gives exactly zero; if unequal, it gives a non-zero minimum — which is why "the dark fringes are not completely dark" when one slit is partly covered.

02 Stationary waves — the wave that goes nowhere

Send a wave down a string, let it reflect off the fixed end, and the incident and reflected waves superpose. At some points they always cancel (nodes); at others they always reinforce (antinodes). Nothing travels — the pattern just stands there and breathes.

Incident + reflected = stationary watch the two thin waves make the thick one
node → node spacing = λ / 2 node → antinode = λ / 4 λ = 2L/3
Formation — the three conditions (write all three)

A stationary wave forms when two progressive waves

1. travel in opposite directions along the same line and overlap,
2. have the same frequency (and hence same wavelength and speed),
3. have equal (or similar) amplitude.

In practice: the wave sent along the medium and its reflection from a fixed boundary.

Node — a point of zero amplitude (destructive superposition at all times).
Antinode — a point of maximum amplitude (constructive superposition).

Traps

Between adjacent nodes every particle is in phase — they all reach maximum displacement at the same instant. They just don't all have the same amplitude. Phase and amplitude are different things; the exam separates them deliberately.

Distance between adjacent nodes is λ/2, not λ. Getting this wrong halves or doubles every answer downstream.

A stationary wave does not transfer energy — but energy is still stored in it, and each particle still has kinetic and potential energy. "No energy" is wrong; "no energy transfer along the wave" is right.

03 Harmonics on strings and in pipes

The boundary decides everything. A fixed end must be a node. A free/open end must be an antinode. Fit whole numbers of quarter-wavelengths between them and you have every possible harmonic.

Closed pipe, harmonic by harmonic: L = λ/4, L = 3λ/4, L = 5λ/4… So a closed pipe of the same length sounds an octave lower than an open one, and it is missing the even harmonics.

Resonance

Resonance — a large-amplitude stationary wave is produced when the driving frequency equals a natural frequency of the system.

Fundamental frequency (first harmonic) — the lowest frequency at which a stationary wave forms in the system.

The resonance-tube experiment (measuring the speed of sound)

A tube is raised out of water under a vibrating tuning fork of known f. You hear resonance at length L₁, then again at L₂.

First resonance: λ/4 = L₁ + e    Second resonance: 3λ/4 = L₂ + e
Subtract to kill the end correction: λ/2 = L₂ − L₁ → v = f × 2(L₂ − L₁).

Why subtract? The antinode sits slightly above the open end by an unknown end correction e. Subtracting eliminates e — that's the whole point of taking two readings. That sentence is a mark.

Fine powder in a horizontal tube (Kundt's tube) piles up at the nodes, because the air there is not moving. Distance between heaps = λ/2.

Trap — the piston question

"A piston is moved along a tube; resonance is heard at piston positions 20 cm apart. Find λ."
The 20 cm is the distance between adjacent nodes ⇒ λ = 40 cm, not 20 cm.

"State the lowest frequency at which resonance occurs" — go back to the fundamental for that system, don't just use the frequency you were given.

04 Diffraction — waves bending round corners

Squeeze a wave through a gap. If the gap is much wider than λ, the wave carries straight on with only a little fringing at the edges. If the gap is about the same size as λ, the wave spreads out into a semicircle. Narrow the gap and watch.

Gap width vs wavelength plan view, like a ripple tank
a / λ = 3.0 gap ≫ λ → little spreading
Definition

Diffraction — the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle/edge.

Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap width is approximately equal to the wavelength.

The wavelength, frequency and speed are unchanged by diffraction. Only the shape of the wavefronts changes.

Drawing the wavefronts — the marking points
  • Draw at least 3 wavefronts after the gap.
  • They must be curved (semicircular for a narrow gap), centred on the gap.
  • Spacing must be unchanged — same distance apart as before the gap (λ doesn't change).
  • For a wide gap: flat in the middle, curling only at the edges.

Longer wavelength ⇒ more diffraction. That is why you can hear round a corner (λ ≈ 1 m) but cannot see round it (λ ≈ 500 nm).

05 Two sources — path difference decides everything

Two coherent sources. Pick any point in the water. Measure how far it is from each source. The difference between those two distances, expressed in wavelengths, tells you instantly whether that point is loud or silent. Drag the point P and watch.

Coherence — the most-asked definition in this chapter

Coherent — having a constant phase difference (which requires the same frequency and hence the same wavelength).

Two sources must be coherent for a stable, observable interference pattern. If the phase difference varies randomly the maxima and minima move about too fast to see and the pattern is washed out.

They should also have similar amplitudes, so that the minima are close to zero and the pattern has good contrast.

Path difference rules
Path differencePhase differencen
Maximum (constructive)nλn × 360°0, 1, 2, 3…

Method for every path-difference question: path difference ÷ λ. Whole number → maximum. Something-and-a-half → minimum.

"Why don't two car headlights make an interference pattern?" [3]
  • The two sources are not coherent — the phase difference between them changes randomly.
  • They emit a range of wavelengths (white light), not a single frequency.
  • Their separation a is large, so x = λD/a is far too small to resolve.

This is why the classic experiment uses one source split into two by a double slit — that guarantees coherence.

06 Young's double-slit experiment

Same physics as section 5, now measured. The bright fringes land at regular spacing x on a screen a distance D away. Change λ, a or D and watch the fringes respond — then check against the formula.

Double slit → fringes on the screen the strip is what the student would actually see
x = λD/a = 2.20 mm minima are completely dark
The formula and its conditions

x = λD / a

x = fringe separation (centre of one bright fringe to the centre of the next), a = slit separation, D = slit-to-screen distance. Valid only when D ≫ a — which is why D is metres and a is a fraction of a millimetre.

Measuring x accurately: measure across many fringes (e.g. 10 gaps) and divide. Measuring one fringe gives a huge percentage uncertainty. Saying this earns the mark.

Trap — units

λ in nanometres, a in millimetres, D in metres, x asked for in millimetres. Convert everything to metres first, then convert the answer at the end. More marks are lost here than on the physics.

07 The diffraction grating

Thousands of slits instead of two. The maxima become razor-sharp and are thrown out to large angles, which is why gratings — not double slits — are used to actually measure wavelengths.

Orders and their angles try white light
d = 1/N = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m highest order nmax = 3 total maxima = 7 —
The grating equation

d sin θ = nλ

d = the grating spacing = distance between the centres of adjacent slits = 1 / N, where N is the number of lines per metre.
n = the order (n = 0 is the straight-through central maximum).
θ = angle of that order from the normal (the straight-through direction).

Counting orders — sin θ can never exceed 1, so nmax = d/λ rounded DOWN to a whole number. Total number of maxima seen = 2nmax + 1 (both sides, plus the central one).

Edge case worth knowing: if d/λ comes out as an exact whole number, that order would need sin θ = 1, i.e. θ = 90° — it travels along the grating and never reaches a screen. So in that one case take one fewer. Almost every exam question gives you a non-integer, where plain rounding down is the whole answer.

Also mind the units: N is usually quoted in lines per mm, so convert first — 500 lines/mm = 5.00 × 10⁵ lines/m, giving d = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m.

Dispersion — which colour goes furthest?

From d sin θ = nλ with d and n fixed: bigger λ ⇒ bigger sin θ ⇒ bigger θ.

Red (longest λ) is diffracted through the largest angle.
Violet/blue (shortest λ) is diffracted through the smallest angle.

This is the opposite way round to a prism, where blue bends most. Examiners love the contrast. In a grating spectrum, the zero order is white because all wavelengths have zero path difference there.

One consequence that surprises people: at high orders the spectra can overlap. With d = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m, violet (430 nm) in the third order sits at 40.2°, while red (680 nm) in the second order sits at 42.8° — so the start of the third-order spectrum appears before the end of the second. Nothing is wrong; the orders simply run into each other. Notice too that red has no third order here (3λ/d = 1.02 > 1), which is why the highest order you can see depends on the colour.

Worked patterns you should recognise instantly

"The angle between the two second-order maxima is 59.4°." That's the angle spanning both sides — so θ for one second order is 29.7°. Then d = 2λ/sin 29.7°. Forgetting to halve is the single commonest slip in grating questions.

"How many orders are visible with a 450 lines/mm grating and 630 nm light?"
d = 1/(450 × 10³) = 2.22 × 10⁻⁶ m. nmax = d/λ = 2.22×10⁻⁶ / 630×10⁻⁹ = 3.53 → 3. Total maxima = 2(3) + 1 = 7.

"…and with blue light instead?" Shorter λ ⇒ d/λ larger ⇒ more orders.

Three sentences worth memorising word for word

1. "A stationary wave is formed by the superposition of two progressive waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions."

2. "Coherent means the two waves have a constant phase difference."

3. "Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle, and is greatest when the gap is comparable to the wavelength."
