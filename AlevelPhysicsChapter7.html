CAIE 9702 · Chapter 7

Waves — seeing the invisible

A wave is energy moving through matter without the matter moving along with it. Every single mark in this chapter comes from being able to picture that sentence. Everything below moves — drag the sliders and watch what the equations actually mean.

01 The two graphs that look identical (and aren't)

This is the #1 mark-loser in the whole chapter. Both graphs are sine curves. Both are labelled "displacement". But one has distance on the x-axis and the other has time. Read λ off the wrong one and you lose every mark that follows.

Wave anatomy — one wave, two graphs, live the red dot is ONE particle
v = fλ = 2.00 m s⁻¹ T = 1/f = 2.00 s particle velocity right now: —
Say it exactly like this

Displacement — the distance and direction of a particle from its equilibrium position. ("and direction" is worth the mark.)

Amplitude — the maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position.

Wavelength — the distance between two adjacent points on a wave that are in phase.

Period — the time taken for one complete oscillation of a particle.

Frequency — the number of complete oscillations per unit time.

Wavefront — a line joining all points on a wave that are in phase.

Trap

If the x-axis says t / ms you can only get T and hence f from it. If it says d / cm you can only get λ. You need both graphs to get v = fλ — which is exactly why the exam gives you two figures side by side.

Check the prefix before you divide. 0.225 ms is 2.25 × 10⁻⁴ s, not 2.25 × 10⁻³. Wrong power of ten = wrong answer with correct working = 1 mark instead of 3.

What they actually ask

"Use Fig. 4.1 and Fig. 4.2 to determine the amplitude, the wavelength, the frequency and the speed." appears in ~1 paper in 2

Order of attack: A from the height of either graph → T from the time graph → f = 1/T → λ from the distance graph → v = fλ. Never guess the order.

02 Transverse vs longitudinal — the same graph, two meanings

A longitudinal wave is drawn as a sine curve too. But that curve is not the shape of anything. It is a bookkeeping graph: positive = particle pushed right, negative = particle pushed left. Watch the dots and the curve at the same time until that clicks.

Particles vs the graph energy always travels →
Transverse: vibration ⊥ to energy transfer Longitudinal: vibration ∥ to energy transfer
Mark-scheme wording

Transverse — the vibrations of the particles are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer of the wave.

Longitudinal — the vibrations of the particles are parallel to the direction of energy transfer of the wave.

Compression — region where particles move towards each other → higher pressure.
Rarefaction — region where particles move away from each other → lower pressure.

Trap — "sketch it later" vs "which way is it moving?"

These are two different questions and they need two different methods. Mixing them up is the single commonest way to lose this mark.

(a) "Sketch the wave a quarter of a period later." Shift the whole curve λ/4 in the direction of travel. That is all. You are drawing a shape, not a particle.

(b) "State the direction of motion of particle Z at this instant." Do not shift the curve and read Z's new displacement — that gives you where Z ends up, not which way it is going now. Use this rule instead:

A particle copies whatever the point just BEHIND it is doing now — "behind" meaning the side the wave came from. For a wave travelling right, look at the curve immediately to the left of Z. If the curve there is higher than Z, then Z is moving up; if lower, Z is moving down. Reverse it for a wave travelling left.

Check it against the shape: a particle sitting at a crest or a trough is momentarily stationary, and a particle at zero displacement is moving fastest. If your answer says a crest is moving, you have made a mistake.

For a longitudinal wave the graph's vertical axis is displacement along the direction of travel, so "up" on the graph means the particle is displaced whichever way the question defines as positive — always read the sign convention the question states (usually "displacement to the right is positive"). The rule above is unchanged.

Never say "the wave moves the particle along". Particles only oscillate about a fixed equilibrium position. No net transfer of matter.

03 Phase difference — it's just a fraction of a cycle × 360°

One cycle ≡ one wavelength ≡ one period ≡ 360° ≡ 2π rad. That identity is the whole topic. Everything else is measuring a gap and dividing.

Two points on one wave · two waves at one point switch mode below
separation Δx = 2.00 m Δx / λ = 0.500 phase difference = 180° antiphase
The only formula you need

φ = (Δx / λ) × 360°   or   φ = (Δt / T) × 360°

Both are "how far apart, as a fraction of one whole cycle". Same idea, different axis.

Traps

Reducing the angle is two steps, and students usually only do the first. Step 1 — throw away whole turns: 1.25λ → 450°, and 450 − 360 = 90°. Step 2 — if what is left is still more than 180°, take 360° minus it, because a lag of 270° and a lead of 90° are the same relationship: 0.75λ → 270° → you report 90°. So a phase difference, properly reduced, is never more than 180°. That is the number the simulation above prints — set Δx/λ to 0.750 and check it.

Points separated by a whole number of wavelengths are in phase (φ = 0). Points on opposite sides of a single node/half-cycle are 180° out of phase.

The exam sometimes asks for the unit — write the degree symbol, or state "rad" if you used π. A bare number scores zero when the question says "include an appropriate unit".

04 Intensity ∝ amplitude²

The single most tested proportionality in the chapter, and the one students square the wrong way round.

Amplitude ⇄ intensity drag either slider
A / A₀ = 1.00 I / I₀ = (A/A₀)² = 1.00
Definitions

Intensity — the power incident on a surface per unit area. I = P / A, unit W m⁻².

For a progressive wave, I ∝ amplitude².

For a point source spreading in 3D, I ∝ 1 / r² — so amplitude ∝ 1 / r.

The four classic one-liners
QuestionAnswerWhy
Amplitude falls to half. New intensity?I₀ / 4(½)²
Intensity doubles. New amplitude?√2 × A₀A ∝ √I
Intensity ¼ of original. New amplitude on the CRO trace?½ × A₀√(¼)

Trap

"Is the wave losing power as it moves away from the source?" — the answer is judged on amplitude decreasing with distance, not on the wave "getting slower". Speed does not change; amplitude does.

05 The CRO — a graph in disguise

A cathode-ray oscilloscope screen is just a displacement–time graph where you are told the scale in seconds per centimetre (time-base) and volts per centimetre (y-gain). Count squares, multiply, done.

CRO reading drill 1 large square = 1.0 cm
Work out T, f and the amplitude, then check.
Method (write it out every time — the working carries marks)
  1. Count the horizontal length of one complete wave in cm.
  2. T = (length in cm) × (time-base in s cm⁻¹)
  3. f = 1 / T
  4. Count from the centre line to a peak in cm (not peak-to-peak!).
  5. amplitude = (height in cm) × (y-gain in V cm⁻¹)
Traps

Peak-to-peak is twice the amplitude. The question almost always wants the amplitude.

Time-base is often in µs cm⁻¹ or ms cm⁻¹. 250 µs cm⁻¹ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ s cm⁻¹.

The microphone converts sound into a varying voltage of the same frequency. The CRO shows the voltage, not the pressure or displacement — but the frequency is identical.

"State three features common to all regions" — [3]

1. They are all transverse waves.
2. They all travel at the same speed 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in free space.
3. They can all travel through a vacuum (and can all be polarised).

If asked for a property not shared with other transverse waves: they can travel through a vacuum.

Trap

Ranges overlap in the real world; give a value safely inside a band. For X-rays say 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, for infra-red say 1 × 10⁻⁵ m. Don't sit on a boundary.

A block diagram labelled "wavelength decreasing →" runs radio → micro → IR → visible → UV → X → gamma. Check the arrow direction before you name anything.

07 Doppler effect — the wavefronts bunch up

The source keeps emitting at the same rate. But it chases the wavefronts it already made, so ahead of it they crowd together (shorter λ → higher f) and behind it they stretch apart. Watch the circles.

Moving source, stationary observer circles = wavefronts, each emitted one period apart
vsound = 340 m s⁻¹ fo = fs · v/(v − vs) = 485 Hz moving towards → pitch rises
Definition and formula

Doppler effect — the change in the observed frequency when there is relative motion between a source and an observer.

fo = fs × v / (v ± vs)

− when the source moves towards the observer (denominator smaller → fo larger).
+ when the source moves away (denominator larger → fo smaller).

The circular-track question recurring

A car circles a track sounding a 1.2 kHz horn; an observer stands far away. The graph of f against time oscillates between about 1.4 kHz and 1.05 kHz.

  • Maximum f — car at the point where it moves directly towards the observer (full vs along the line of sight).
  • Minimum f — directly away.
  • f = fs exactly (1.2 kHz) — the two points where the car moves perpendicular to the line of sight: vs along the line of sight is zero.
  • To find the car's speed, use the maximum (or minimum) frequency, where the whole speed is along the line of sight.
Trap

The syllabus formula only handles a moving source. If the observer moves instead, you're outside the AS formula — the exam won't ask for a number, only a direction of change.

The wave speed does not change. What changes is the wavelength in front of / behind the source, and therefore the frequency detected.

08 Polarisation and Malus's law

Unpolarised light vibrates in every plane perpendicular to travel. A polarising filter throws away everything except the component along its transmission axis. Rotate the second filter and watch the cos² curve draw itself.

Two filters + intensity meter drag θ and watch the graph build
after filter 1: 4.0 W m⁻² (= ½ Iunpol) after filter 2: I₁cos²θ = 4.0 W m⁻²
Definitions

Plane-polarised — the oscillations of the wave are in one plane only, which contains the direction of propagation (i.e. the vibration is restricted to a single direction perpendicular to the direction of travel).

Polarisation — the process of restricting the oscillations of a wave to a single plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Malus's law: I = I₀ cos²θ, where θ is the angle between the plane of the incident polarised wave and the transmission axis of the filter.

Two questions that come up again and again

"Why can light be polarised but sound cannot?"
Light is a transverse wave, so its vibrations occur in many planes perpendicular to the direction of travel and can be restricted to one. Sound is longitudinal — the vibration is already along the direction of travel, so there are no sideways planes to select from. Only transverse waves can be polarised.

"Sketch I against angle as the filter is rotated through 360°"
Starting from a maximum: maxima at 0°, 180°, 360°; zeros at 90° and 270°. Two full humps. Curve must never go negative and must touch zero (not just dip).

Trap — the chained-filter angle

θ in Malus's law is measured between the light's plane of polarisation and the next filter's axis — not from the vertical.

Worked example: filter 1 axis at 35° to vertical, vertically polarised light of 8.5 W m⁻² in.
After filter 1: I₁ = 8.5 cos²(35°) = 5.7 W m⁻², and the light is now polarised at 35°.
Filter 2 gives 5.2 W m⁻²: cos²θ = 5.2/5.7 → θ = 17.2° from filter 1's axis.
So α = 35° + 17.2° = 52.2° from the vertical. Missing that last addition is the classic drop.

Unpolarised light through the first filter always loses exactly half its intensity, whatever the angle. Malus's law does not apply to that first step.

The drawing questions — a checklist that scores full marks

"Draw a second wave with the same amplitude but half the frequency" → same height, twice the wavelength on a distance axis (or twice the period on a time axis). Keep it starting at the same point.

"Draw a wave with double the intensity, 180° out of phase" → amplitude × √2, and inverted (peaks where the original has troughs).

"Sketch the wave 0.25 T later" → shift the whole curve a quarter of a wavelength in the direction of travel. Do not change its shape, height or wavelength.

"Sketch the CRO trace when the intensity is quartered" → the trace height halves (√¼ = ½). The horizontal spacing does not change — the CRO's x-axis is time, so what you see repeating across the screen is the period, not the wavelength, and the frequency is unchanged.
